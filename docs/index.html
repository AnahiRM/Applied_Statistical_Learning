Predicting energy consumption in France: when the model is right on average but wrong where it matters

By Anahí Reyes Miguel

Grid operators must anticipate electricity demand in real time. Over-prediction wastes resources, meanwhile nder-prediction risks blackouts. In a country as climatically and demographically diverse as France, a model that performs well on average may still fail systematically in specific areas.

We trained five different models on daily weather observations and population density data to predict electricity consumption per capita across 12 metropolitan French regions from 2017 to 2021. This is the story of what we found.

Section 2 — The model

Choosing the right architecture

We trained our candidate models on a robust dataset of 25,390 daily observations across 12 French regions. While the Neural Network achieved a marginally higher R2, the Random Forest was selected for its balance of predictive power and its ability to rank feature importance.

The twist

Average performance conceals critical spatial heterogeneity

When we decompose prediction errors by region, a different picture emerges. A one-way ANOVA confirms that regional differences in prediction error are statistically significant — F=10.03, p<0.001. These are not random fluctuations. The gap between the best and worst predicted region is 52%.

Applying a national-level model with uniform assumptions across regions obscures these critical variations — exactly the kind of aggregation bias that could make regional energy planning unreliable.

Section 5 — Interpretation

Why are some regions harder to predict?

One might assume that denser, more urban regions should be easier to predict. The logic is intuitive: high-density areas favor collective housing with shared walls, which are thermally more efficient than detached suburban homes. This should lead to lower, more stable per-capita heating demand.

The data proves this hypothesis wrong. Île-de-France is the most densely populated region in France (1,009 people/km²) yet it exhibits the highest prediction error. Urban morphology, rather than just climate, offers an alternative explanation. As explored by Lafourcade et al. (2021), the relationship between urban compactness and environmental footprints is non-linear.

Fig. 4 — Mean MAPE vs population density (log scale). The "density-reliability" hypothesis is rejected: Île-de-France is both the densest and the least predictable region.

In dense urban centers like Paris, the model suffers from typological blindness. A weather-based model sees a drop in temperature and predicts a sharp rise in heating demand. However, residents in well-insulated, shared-wall apartment blocks require substantially less energy than the "average" French home. Because the model lacks data on building typology, it systematically overestimates demand in these zones.

Beyond physical structures, urban centers introduce unique behavioral and economic noise. The "split-incentive" or landlord-tenant problem—where the party responsible for insulation upgrades does not pay the energy bills—creates vast discrepancies in building efficiency that weather data alone cannot capture (Petrov & Ryan, 2021).

Additionally, the permanent shift toward hybrid work has decoupled consumption from traditional geographic patterns. As noted by Smite et al. (2023), work-from-home policies have redistributed energy load from predictable commercial hubs to dispersed residential circuits, introducing a level of randomness that current regional models struggle to resolve.

In contrast, regions with stable Mediterranean climates, such as Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur, could be benefiting from more consistent cooling and heating patterns that a weather-driven Random Forest can learn with high precision.

"Predictive reliability is not merely a function of climate and population density. It is a reflection of urban form and human behavior—the complex (non-linear) filters through which weather is translated into energy demand."
Section 6 — Conclusion

Understanding where models fail is as important as their average performance

Our Random Forest achieves strong average performance (R²=0.941), but its reliability varies significantly across French regions, and those variations are statistically significant and spatially structured. Better regional forecasting requires not just better weather data, but a more comprehensive dataset that includes data on building typology, housing tenure, and urban morphology.

This analysis shifts the focus from predictive accuracy to predictive reliability across space: a distinction that matters for grid operators, urban planners, and anyone making decisions based on regional energy forecasts.
