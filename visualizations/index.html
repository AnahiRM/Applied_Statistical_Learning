Predicting energy consumption in France — when the model is right on average but wrong where it matters

Grid operators must anticipate electricity demand in real time. Over-prediction wastes resources. Under-prediction risks blackouts. In a country as climatically and demographically diverse as France, a model that performs well on average may still fail systematically in specific places.

We trained a Random Forest model on daily weather observations and regional demographic data to predict electricity consumption per capita across 12 metropolitan French regions from 2017 to 2021. This is the story of what we found.

The twist

Average performance conceals critical spatial heterogeneity

When we decompose prediction errors by region, a very different picture emerges. A one-way ANOVA confirms that regional differences in prediction error are statistically significant — F=10.03, p<0.001. These are not random fluctuations. The gap between the best and worst predicted region is 52%.

Applying a national-level model with uniform assumptions across regions obscures these critical variations — exactly the kind of aggregation bias that makes regional energy planning unreliable.

Section 5 — Interpretation

Why are some regions harder to predict?

The naive hypothesis — that denser, more urban regions should be easier to predict — is wrong. Île-de-France is the most dense region in France (1,009 people/km²) and has the highest prediction error. Urban economics offers the explanation.

Dense urban areas like Paris are dominated by collective housing — apartment buildings with shared walls, which are thermally far more efficient than detached single-family homes. A weather-based model sees cold temperatures and predicts high heating demand. But Parisians in well-insulated apartment blocks need substantially less heating than the model expects. The model is blind to building typology, and this blindness is systematic.

Beyond building form, several structural factors introduce inherent unpredictability: the split-incentive problem between landlords and tenants, older building stock near city centres, and the COVID-19 shift to telework which permanently redistributed demand from predictable commercial districts to dispersed residential suburbs.

Regions with stable Mediterranean climates — Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur and Nouvelle-Aquitaine — benefit from consistent consumption patterns that a weather-based model learns well.

"Prediction reliability is not simply a function of climate variability. It reflects the urban form, building stock, and behavioral patterns that determine how regions translate weather into energy demand."
Section 6 — Conclusion

Understanding where models fail is as important as their average performance

Our Random Forest achieves strong average performance (R²=0.941), but its reliability varies significantly across French regions — and those variations are statistically significant and spatially structured. Better regional forecasting requires not just better weather data, but data on building typology, housing tenure, and urban morphology.

This analysis shifts the focus from predictive accuracy to predictive reliability across space — a distinction that matters for grid operators, urban planners, and anyone making decisions based on regional energy forecasts.

"While our model predicts energy consumption well on average, its performance varies significantly across regions — revealing that the relationship between weather and energy consumption is mediated by urban structure in ways that aggregate models cannot capture."
